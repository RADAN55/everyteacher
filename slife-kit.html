A newcomer who is also new to school

SLIFE stands for students with limited or interrupted formal education. Most newcomers are learning English on top of a full education. A SLIFE student is learning English, reading and writing, grade-level content and how school works, all at once, usually as a teenager with very little time.

What it is

A schooling history, not an ability

The student missed school because of war, migration, work, distance or cost. Nothing about that says how fast the student can learn now.

What it is not

Not a disability, not a placement

Limited schooling is not a reason for a special education referral, and it is not a reason to place a sixteen-year-old in a middle school.

What it takes

A second layer of support

Everything in the Newcomer Series still applies. This kit adds what a newcomer plan assumes and a SLIFE student does not yet have.

No federal definition. Federal law does not define SLIFE, and states that define it do so differently; a common marker is two or more years below grade level in home-language literacy or math because of missed schooling. Confirm whether your district's EL plan has its own definition or flag before you record the term anywhere official.

Find out, in the first week

You cannot plan for a schooling gap you do not know about, and the paperwork will not tell you. Three small things in the first week: an interview, a home-language sample, and a numbers check.

1 · The schooling interview

Twelve questions, with an interpreter or a trusted bilingual adult. Sit beside the student, not across. Ask about school and skills only. Do not ask how or why the family came to the United States, and never about immigration status.

2 · A home-language sample

Ask the student to write for five minutes in the language they know best: "Tell me about your family," or "Tell me about a day at your last school." You do not need to read the language to learn from it. Look at how much was written, how fast, whether there are sentences and punctuation, and how the pencil is held. Then ask a bilingual adult to read it. If the student says they do not write, ask them to read a short text aloud in the home language instead, and note it.

3 · A numbers check with no English

One page of bare computation that climbs: counting and place value, the four operations, fractions and decimals, a one-step equation. No word problems. Stop when the student stops. Many SLIFE students compute well mentally; ask them to show you how they got an answer in their own way.

Signs to look for

Keep it out of the wrong files. The interview notes belong with the EL records. They are not a disability screening. If a real disability concern appears later, the Dual-Identified Desk covers how to tell the difference.

The first 30 days

The Newcomer Week 1 Kit covers the first five days for any new arrival. For a SLIFE student, add these.

  1. Week 1: belong and learn the building. One adult the student can find. One peer guide. Walk the schedule twice. Teach the routines out loud that other students absorbed years ago: the bell, the tardy, the bathroom pass, the cafeteria line, the Chromebook login. Do the interview and the two checks.
  2. Week 2: the tools of school. How to head a paper, keep a notebook, copy from the board, read a schedule, use a planner. Start the first 100 school words with pictures and sound. Begin daily literacy at the level the home-language sample showed.
  3. Week 3: daily literacy and numeracy are fixed in the schedule. A protected block for learning to read, with age-appropriate material, every day. Math placement set from the numbers check, not from age or English. Content teachers have the one-page note from this kit.
  4. Week 4: the plan on paper. LSP written with the schooling history in it. Counselor meeting: credits, age, graduation path. Family conference with an interpreter. First small success made public: a labeled poster, a read-aloud of three sentences, a solved problem at the board.
Measure the right thing. In the first month, progress is: comes every day, knows where to go, has an adult and a friend, can write name and date, recognizes fifty words, attempts every task. Put those in the notes. Grades come later.

How to teach a student who is new to school

Six principles that hold up across the guidance and the research.

1 · Start from what the student can do

Many SLIFE students have run a market stall, raised siblings, repaired engines or farmed. Begin a unit with a task that draws on that knowledge, then attach the school version of it.

2 · Talk first, print second

The student's strength is oral. Discuss, act out and sketch the idea before any reading. Then read a short text about what was just discussed.

3 · Teach reading directly, with dignity

A teenager who is learning to decode needs explicit daily instruction in sounds and print, with material that does not look like kindergarten. The Storm Phonics Lab was built for this.

4 · Teach the school kind of thinking

Sorting into categories, defining a word, comparing two things on a chart, answering a question whose answer the teacher already knows: these are school habits, and they are learned. Show how each one is done.

5 · Make it matter today

Reading a pay stub, a bus schedule, a lease, a medicine label. Immediate use builds the habit of reading, and the academic content can ride on it.

6 · Work together, then alone

Many students come from settings where learning is shared and shown, not written and individual. Let the group produce the first version. Move to individual, written work in steps.

One framework worth reading. DeCapua and Marshall's Mutually Adaptive Learning Paradigm describes exactly this bridge: accept the conditions students need (relevance and relationships), combine their ways of learning with the school's (oral with written, shared with individual), and teach school-style tasks using familiar content.

Schedule and credits

The schedule is where a SLIFE student is most often failed. Two mistakes: a full load of grade-level classes with no foundation block, or a day of electives and study hall "until English comes."

Build the day like this

  • An EL block every day, with foundational literacy inside it
  • Math at the level the numbers check showed, with a path to Algebra I
  • One or two credit-bearing classes with strong visuals and hands-on work: CTE, art, PE, a lab science with support
  • Age-appropriate placement. A sixteen-year-old belongs in high school.
  • The same adult checking in weekly

Tell the family the truth about time

  • A diploma is possible, and it may take longer than four years.
  • Under Mississippi law a resident may attend public school until age 21. Confirm the district's practice for 18 to 20-year-olds.
  • Summer terms and credit recovery are planned from the first semester.
  • Steering a student to a high school equivalency program because of English or missed schooling is not a plan.

For classroom teachers

A one-page note for the content teacher who has a SLIFE student on the roster. Print it and hand it over with the student's profile.

A student who is new to school

This student is learning English and has also missed years of schooling. That means reading and writing are still being learned, in any language, and school routines are new. It does not mean the student cannot learn your subject. Expect a capable, practical young person who has not yet been taught how school works.

You may seeWhat helps
Copies slowly, or does not take notesGive a partly filled page: the student adds five words and a sketch.
Does not start a worksheetDo the first item together. Show a finished example.
Silent in whole-class talkAsk in a pair first. Accept a pointed-to answer or one word.
Knows the idea, cannot read the textShow it, say it, then give two sentences to read about it.
Test left blankAsk the three most important questions aloud, or with pictures.

Five moves for any subject: seat the student with a kind, steady partner; put a picture next to every key word; give one instruction at a time and show it; let the student show learning by doing, drawing or saying; notice one thing done right each day and say it.

Grade what the student can show of your content with these supports. The EL teacher will help you set fair targets. Tell the EL teacher about attendance changes, signs of distress, or sudden progress.
