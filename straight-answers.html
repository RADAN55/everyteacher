Straight answers.

The forty questions content teachers actually ask about English learners, answered in a hundred words each: the answer, the rule behind it where there is one, and the tool on this hub that finishes the job. Search, or read the seven that apply to Monday.

1I have an EL in my class and I don't teach English. What do I actually do on Monday?

Three things, about two minutes each. Post a language goal next to your content objective ("explain the cause using because"). Hand out one sentence frame for the task. Let students talk to a partner before anyone writes or answers in front of the class. That is most of the gap for most ELs, and none of it costs content time. Then find out the student's four ELPT levels — the EL teacher has them — and read what they mean for your class.

2What do the levels on my roster mean?

The ELPT scores Listening, Speaking, Reading and Writing from 1 to 5 every spring. A 1 understands a few words with pictures; a 3 follows a familiar lesson and writes a paragraph; a 5 is at grade level. Students exit when every domain is 4 or higher. The lowest domain — almost always Reading or Writing — is the one to plan the task around. The level measures English, not intelligence or content knowledge.

The rule: Mississippi exit standard: Level 4+ in all four domains (State Board, June 2025).

3He speaks English fine in the hallway. Why is he still an EL?

Because the hallway and the textbook are two different languages. Social English arrives in one to two years; the English of a lab report, a word problem or a primary source takes five to seven. A Speaking 4 with a Reading 2 is the most common profile in a high school. Trust the Reading and Writing numbers for schoolwork, and give the student grade-level text with the key words previewed rather than easier text.

4She's been here three years and still can't write a paragraph. Is she just not trying?

Almost never. Interrupted schooling, limited literacy in the home language, a late start, or years of being allowed to answer in spoken English all produce the same picture, and none of them is a choice the student made. Three years at Writing 2 is a reason to look at the plan — is anyone teaching the academic register explicitly? — not at the student. Require a paragraph with a frame, every day, and mark one feature.

5How many ELs should be in one class? Is it better to spread them out?

Cluster them. Two or more ELs in a section gives each one a partner for structured talk and gives you one set of scaffolds to prepare; one EL alone in a room has nobody to rehearse with and often goes silent. The counselor should also put ELs in sections where the teacher has had the four-lane PD. Spreading them thin feels fair and works worst.

6The newcomer hasn't said a word in three weeks. Should I be worried?

No. A silent period of weeks to months is normal for a new arrival; the student is listening and understanding more each day. Keep including them: seat near a bilingual peer, give tasks that can be done by pointing, drawing, labeling or sorting, say their name correctly and often, and accept answers in the home language. Worry if the silence comes with distress, not with watching.

7Can I take off points for grammar?

On a non-ELA assignment, no — a grammar penalty measures English, not your subject, and the student already takes a test of English every spring. Score the content: did the student show the concept, in words, a diagram, a label or aloud? Give language feedback separately, on one feature at a time. In ELA, where writing conventions are the standard, grade the feature you taught rather than every error.

The rule: The LSP's grading section; Lau v. Nichols; EEOA.

8How do I grade a newcomer who can't read the test?

Change the evidence, not the standard. Read the questions aloud (allowed; not the passages on state tests), accept a labeled diagram, a list, a sequence, an oral answer or an answer in Spanish for a content question. Two choices instead of four on multiple choice. If the student cannot show the content any of those ways, the content is not there yet, and that is a true F — but most newcomers can.

9Isn't it unfair to the other students if ELs get easier work?

They don't get easier work; they get the same standard with different evidence. A Newcomer proves "explains the cause of the war" with a timeline and six words; a native speaker proves it with a paragraph. Both met or missed the standard. What would be unfair is grading a student on English in a biology class — and, in the other direction, a courtesy C that hides a gap.

10Can an EL be retained or fail a grade because of English?

Not for language reasons alone. If the student is failing because content is not being learned despite the supports in the plan, that is a program question first — are the supports actually happening? — and then a grade question. The Language Service Plan has a section on grading and retention for exactly this moment; read it with the EL teacher before any decision.

The rule: Castañeda v. Pickard; the Mississippi LSP.

11Do I have to give extra time on everything?

If extended time is in the student's Language Service Plan, yes, on assessments — and it works better given up front than as a favor after the bell. On classwork, shorten the task instead of lengthening the day: odd-numbered problems, one paragraph instead of three. Extended time is also the single most common state-test accommodation, and it only counts there if the student uses it routinely in class.

The rule: MTAM #20.

12What is a Language Service Plan and why do I have to sign it?

The LSP is Mississippi's written plan for each EL: identification trail, ELPT levels, services, classroom and testing accommodations, and the grading and retention rules that apply. You sign because you are one of the people delivering it; the accommodations in it are obligations, not suggestions, and the testing office honors only what the plan says. Ask the EL teacher for the one-page version for your folder.

13Am I allowed to change the test?

Your own tests, yes — read-aloud, word banks, frames, fewer choices, oral answers, a leveled version. State tests, only what the Testing Accommodations Manual allows and only what is already in the LSP: extended time, directions read and clarified, questions read aloud (not passages), a print word-to-word dictionary on MAAP, small group, breaks. Anything introduced on test day is both useless and non-compliant.

The rule: Mississippi Testing Accommodations Manual (Feb 2026).

14Can she use Google Translate on the test?

Not on MAAP or the ELPT. The home language is not permitted on the ELPT, and on MAAP the only bilingual support is a print word-to-word dictionary with no definitions or pictures, and only when it is in the plan. In class, a translation app for understanding a passage is fine; for producing writing to be turned in, it is not — the writing would not be the student's.

The rule: MTAM #81.

15What's the ELPT and do I have anything to do with it?

The ELPT (ELPA21) is the English proficiency test every EL takes each spring in four domains. You matter to it more than you think: the reading and writing domains, where most students are stuck, are built in content classes — grade-level text every day, writing with a frame, one feature of feedback. The EL teacher runs the prep; you provide the daily practice.

16Do ELs have to take the English II / Algebra I state tests?

Yes. ELs take every state assessment with their LSP accommodations; the one exception in federal law is that a student in their first twelve months in U.S. schools may be exempt from one administration of the ELA test (not math or science). A failed end-of-course test has options — retakes and combined measures — and never by itself means no diploma.

The rule: ESSA §1111(b)(3).

17When does a student stop being an EL?

When the spring ELPT shows Level 4 or higher in all four domains. The student then exits services and is monitored for four years; grades in your class are part of that monitoring, and a monitored student who starts slipping gets a conversation and supports, not a new label. Students who decline services remain ELs and still test every year.

The rule: State Board exit standard, June 19, 2025.

18I don't speak Spanish. How can I use their Spanish without translating my class?

You don't translate; you let students think and talk in any language and write in English. Point out cognates in your own vocabulary list (most science words are). Let partners plan in Spanish before writing in English. Allow bilingual notes and a translation app for understanding. Learn to say students' names correctly and ten classroom phrases. None of that requires you to speak Spanish; all of it uses what the students already have.

19Should I stop students from speaking Spanish to each other in class?

No — and a rule against it is a civil-rights problem as well as a teaching mistake. Students who explain the content to each other in Spanish are learning the content; the English comes in the written product and the whole-class share. Set the expectation by task: "plan in any language, write in English, present in English with your frame."

The rule: Title VI.

20What's a sentence frame and isn't it cheating?

A frame is the shape of an academic sentence with the content left blank: "___ increased because ___, which shows ___." It is a scaffold, not an answer; the student still has to know the content to fill it. Frames turn silence into participation at Levels 1–3 and teach the register that Levels 3–4 are missing. Fade them as the student stops needing them.

21How do I pick the five words to pre-teach?

Not the hardest words — the ones the lesson cannot be understood without, that students will meet again, and that are not cognates (cognates teach themselves once pointed out). Usually that is two or three content words and two or three general academic words (analyze, therefore, structure). Say each, show it, give a picture or a Spanish bridge, and use it in a frame.

22The textbook is too hard for my Level 2s. Do I give them an easier book?

Give them the real one, with the five key words previewed, a purpose for reading, a diagram, and the questions read aloud — and, for a Level 1–2, a short leveled version beside it that keeps the vocabulary and the diagram. An easier book alone is how students stay at Level 2 for six years: they can read the easy version; it is the grade-level one they need practice in.

23What is "academic language" and why does everyone keep saying it?

The English of textbooks and tests: long noun phrases ("the rate of change of the function"), nominalizations (migrate → migration), passive voice, hedges (may, suggests), and connectives (whereas, consequently). Nobody speaks it at home, in any language, so every student has to be taught it — ELs most explicitly. It is what separates a Level 3 from a Level 4, and it is teachable one feature at a time.

24Is a translation app ever okay?

For understanding, yes: a newcomer reading your slide through a phone is learning your content. For producing work to be turned in, no — the writing is the app's, not the student's, and it teaches nothing. For anything a family signs or decides, never; that needs an interpreter. Say the rule once, by task, and it mostly solves itself.

25How do I contact a parent who doesn't speak English?

Through the office's interpreter line — phone or video, free to the family, booked a day ahead for a call and a week ahead for a meeting. Not the student, a sibling or a classmate for anything that matters. Written notes go home in the family's language; the parent-letter templates cover the formal ones and the conference kit covers the rest. Good news first: a two-minute good-news call changes the whole year.

The rule: Title VI language access.

26What should I say at a parent conference about an EL?

What the child knows first, then the four levels in one sentence each, then one thing the family can do at home in their own language. The sentence that matters: "The grade shows what your child knows about biology, not how much English they have." Families of ELs have often been told for years that their child is behind. Send a summary home on paper, in both languages.

27Should I tell the family to speak English at home?

No — tell them the opposite. A strong home language is the foundation academic English is built on; families who switch to broken English at home take away the one place the child was getting rich language. Ask them to talk about school, read together and discuss the news in Spanish (or Vietnamese, or Arabic). Strong Spanish makes strong English.

28Can I ask a student about their immigration status, or their family's?

No, and neither can the office. Every child has the right to enroll and attend regardless of status; schools may not ask for a Social Security number or immigration documents as a condition of anything. If a student brings it up, listen, keep it private, and point them to the counselor. Do not write it down anywhere.

The rule: Plyler v. Doe (1982).

29A family just arrived and the student starts tomorrow. What do I need?

From the office: the student's name as the family writes it, the home language, and whether the Home Language Survey went to the EL teacher. From the EL teacher within a few weeks: screener levels and a plan. From yourself tomorrow: a seat near a bilingual peer, a visual schedule, a labeled map of the room, a name said right, and a task the student can do by doing. The first week has a kit.

30The EL never participates in discussion. How do I get her to talk?

Change the structure, not the student. Partner talk before whole-class, every time; a frame on the board; wait time of five to seven seconds; then call on her with a question she has already rehearsed with the partner. Cold-calling a Level 2 in front of thirty people produces silence and teaches silence. Rehearsed talk produces a sentence, and the second one is easier.

31Other students laugh at his accent. What do I do?

Treat it as what it is — harassment on the basis of national origin — the first time, publicly and briefly: it stops now. Then, separately, make the student's language an asset in the room: a cognate he can teach, a word in his language on the board, a bilingual text. Students mock what the room treats as a deficit and respect what the room treats as expertise.

The rule: Title VI.

32Is it okay to ask a bilingual student to translate for a classmate?

For a quick direction or a hallway question, yes, and it helps both. As a standing job, no: the bilingual student loses their own lesson, the newcomer never has to try, and anything about grades, health, discipline or family needs a trained interpreter. Rotate the peer support, keep it to the first weeks, and thank the helper out loud.

33Can I put the EL in the back with a worksheet while I teach the rest?

No. That is exclusion from instruction, however kind the worksheet. The student belongs in the lesson with the lanes — a visual, a frame, a partner, a task that shows the content another way. If that feels impossible for a Level 1 in a lecture-heavy class, the fix is the lecture: slides, a diagram, chunking, checks — which every other student also needs.

The rule: Castañeda prong 2.

34What does the law actually require of me, personally?

To deliver the supports in the student's Language Service Plan, to give the student meaningful access to your content, not to exclude or penalize for language, and to communicate with the family in a language they understand through the school's interpreters. That is it. Everything else on this hub is about doing those four things well rather than minimally.

The rule: Lau v. Nichols; EEOA; Castañeda; Title VI; ESSA.

35Who decides a student is an EL, and can a parent say no?

The Home Language Survey at enrollment flags a student; the ELPT screener, given within thirty days of the start of school or two weeks of later enrollment, decides. Parents are notified in their language and may decline services — but a student whose parents decline is still an EL: still tested yearly, still entitled to accommodations and to your support.

The rule: ESSA §1112(e)(3).

36Can an EL be in honors, AP, CTE or athletics?

Yes, and excluding one for language reasons is the first thing a federal review looks for. Place by content: a Level 2 who is strong in math belongs in Honors Geometry with an LSP, not in a study hall. CTE is often the best fit a newcomer has. The level sets the scaffolds, never the ceiling.

The rule: 2015 USDOJ/OCR Dear Colleague Letter.

37Is there a difference between an EL with an IEP and other ELs?

An EL with a disability has both plans, and they must agree. Language level is considered in the special-education evaluation so that English is not mistaken for a disability — or a disability missed because of English. The IEP meeting has an interpreter; IEP and LSP accommodations are aligned. If you see a mismatch, say so to both case managers.

The rule: IDEA; DCL 2015 §I.F.

38What is Title III money and can it buy my class anything?

Federal funds a district receives per EL, for services beyond what the district must already provide — supplemental, never supplanting. They often pay for the EL teacher's materials, PD, family engagement and summer programs. Ask the EL coordinator; a request for bilingual glossaries or leveled science texts for your room is exactly what the money is for.

The rule: ESSA §3115.

39How long should it take a student to exit? When should I worry?

Four to seven years to Proficient is typical; reading and writing come last. Worry when a student has been at the same level for two springs in a row, when a student six or more years in program is still an EL, or when oral English is far ahead of literacy — those are the students the clinic is for. A Level 3 in year three is on schedule.
