Words that trip

Six families of language that cost English learners points on problems they can compute.

FamilyExamplesWhy it trips
Reversal wordsless than · fewer than · subtracted from · divided intoThe order in the sentence is the opposite of the order in the expression. "6 less than a number" is n − 6.
Everyday words with a math meaningtable · mean · odd · even · volume · product · difference · power · root · round · termThe student knows the word and is sure of the wrong meaning. Name both meanings out loud.
Small words that carry the operationof · per · each · by · at · for everyNewcomers skip function words. In math they are the instruction.
Comparisonsat least · at most · no more than · how many more · as many as · twice as many asEnglish comparison grammar is long and the meaning hangs on one word.
Sound-alikessum / some · whole / hole · eight / ate · plane / plain · four / for · two / to / tooA listening problem, so say the word and write it at the same time.
Conditions and passivesif … then · given that · is divided by · was increased by · when x isThe sentence structure hides who is doing what to which number.

Words your Spanish speakers already own

Much of school math vocabulary comes from Latin, so it has a near twin in Spanish. Say so. It is the fastest vocabulary lesson there is.

equation · ecuación, fraction · fracción, triangle · triángulo, angle · ángulo, parallel · paralelo, multiply · multiplicar, divide · dividir, graph · gráfica, variable · variable, exponent · exponente, polygon · polígono, negative · negativo, probability · probabilidad, coordinate · coordenada, linear · lineal, radius · radio, diameter · diámetro, percent · por ciento

Watch for: "razón" is both ratio and reason; "billion" is 1,000,000,000 in U.S. English, while "billón" in Spanish is a million millions; many countries write 3.500,25 where the U.S. writes 3,500.25.

Three Reads

A widely used routine for word problems. The class reads the same problem three times, each time for one purpose. Nobody computes until the third read is done.

  1. Read 1: What is this about? Teacher reads aloud, problem hidden or question covered. Students tell a partner the situation in their own words, in any language. No numbers yet.
  2. Read 2: What are the quantities? Read together. Students list what can be counted or measured, with units: "price of one ticket, in dollars." Record the list on the board.
  3. Read 3: What is the question? Uncover the question. Students restate it starting with "We need to find…" and say which quantities will help.
  4. Then solve. Any method. Partners first, then alone.

The same routine, four lanes

NewcomerDraws or acts out the situation after Read 1. Labels the drawing with numbers after Read 2. A partner who shares the home language retells.
EmergingUses the frame "This problem is about ___." Lists quantities from a word bank with units.
ProgressingRestates the question in a full sentence and names the operation with a reason: "I will ___ because ___."
ChallengeWrites a second question the same situation could answer, and trades it with a partner.
Ten minutes, not thirty. Use it on one problem a day, the one with the most language. The rest of the set goes faster because of it.

Vocabulary in six minutes

Five words a week is enough. Pick the words the unit cannot be done without, and teach each one the same way every time.

  1. Say it, show it (1 min). Say the word, write it, have the class say it twice. Point to a worked example, not a definition.
  2. Student-friendly meaning (1 min). One sentence in plain English. Add the Spanish twin if there is one.
  3. Example and non-example (1 min). "This is a factor of 12. This is not. Why?"
  4. Draw or symbol (1 min). Students sketch it or write it in symbols in a four-square: word, meaning, picture, example.
  5. Use it (2 min). One spoken sentence to a partner with a frame, then one written. The word goes on the wall with its picture.
Then make them use it. For the rest of the week, an answer that could have used the word and did not gets the reply: "Say that again with the word quotient."

Fair assessment in math

A fair math test for an English learner measures the same math and removes English that is not part of the standard.

Change this

  • Shorten the sentences around the numbers. Keep the numbers and the question.
  • Replace rare everyday words (a "tollbooth," a "raffle") with common ones, or add a small picture.
  • Allow a word-to-word bilingual dictionary or glossary if the LSP lists it.
  • Read the directions aloud.
  • Accept an answer shown in a labeled diagram, a table, or steps with short phrases.

Do not change this

  • The standard being tested.
  • The difficulty of the numbers or the number of steps.
  • The math vocabulary the standard names. Teach it; do not delete it.
  • The expectation that the student shows reasoning.
Grade the math and the English separately. If the explanation is mathematically right and grammatically rough, the math score is full. Note the language for the EL teacher.
