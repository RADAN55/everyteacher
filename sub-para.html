Substitute card: “English learners in this room”

A sub meets your ELs cold. This card tells them what to do in the first minute and what not to do all period, without exposing a single student's identity.

Why no names

Counts by lane, not names

A sub card travels: it gets photographed, left on a desk, picked up by students. Lane counts and locations tell the sub everything they need to act. The seating chart you already leave does the rest.

Where to leave it

Clip it to the front of the sub folder, above the seating chart. One card per class period.

Make it a routine

Reprint when a lane count changes (a newcomer arrives, someone exits). The EL teacher can keep the master copy.

Do this

  • Greet them. Smile. Say your name slowly and write it on the board.
  • Give directions in two ways: say it, then show it (point, model, write the steps).
  • Let them use what's on the list below. They are allowed, not cheating.
  • Pair them with the buddy on the seating chart for anything with reading.
  • Accept a drawing, a label, a short answer, a yes/no as evidence they understood.
  • Wait 5 seconds after a question. Then ask again, shorter.

Allowed supports

    Please don't

    • Call on a Newcomer to read aloud or answer in front of the class.
    • Speak louder instead of slower and simpler.
    • Take away the dictionary or the word bank “to be fair.”
    • Mark a student absent-minded or defiant for silence. Silence is often listening.
    • Hand a Newcomer a worksheet in English and walk away.

    For paraprofessionals, tutors, and volunteers

    Working one-on-one with an English learner

    Your job in one sentence: help the student do the thinking in English, a little more each day. Not to do the work, and not to translate everything.

    Know the lane

    LaneThey can…So you…
    NewcomerUnderstand with pictures and gestures; say words and memorized phrases.Point and name. Use the picture dictionary. Ask yes/no and either/or questions. Let them draw or label.
    EmergingMake short sentences with a starter; read short familiar text.Give a sentence frame and wait. Read the first paragraph together, then they read the second.
    ProgressingDo grade-level work with vocabulary help and time.Pre-teach the five hardest words. Model one example, then step back.
    ChallengeNearly everything. Needs academic precision.Push for the exact word. Ask “Say that like a scientist would.”

    Eight moves that work

    1. Wait. Count to five after every question. Then ask again, shorter.
    2. Point and say. Touch the word, the picture, the number while you say it.
    3. Echo, then stretch. Student: “Plant need water.” You: “Yes, the plant needs water. Why does the plant need water?”
    4. Frame it. “I think ___ because ___.” Write it on a sticky note and leave it there.
    5. Use cognates. Observe = observar, compare = comparar, evidence = evidencia. Spanish speakers know more academic English than they think.
    6. Chunk the task. Cover everything except the first problem. Uncover the next when the first is done.
    7. Home language is a tool. Let them think or explain in their language first, then say it in English.
    8. Praise the specific. “You used ‘because.’ That's academic English.”

    Please don't

    • Translate the whole task. Translate one direction word, then let them try.
    • Finish their sentence. The pause is where the learning happens.
    • Correct every error. Fix the one that blocks meaning; let the rest go today.
    • Do the work “so they don't fall behind.” A blank with effort beats a filled page they didn't think about.
    • Ask “Do you understand?” They'll say yes. Ask them to show you instead.

    A 20-minute session

    MinDo
    0–3Greet, ask one real question about their day, preview today's words (3–5) with pictures.
    3–12The class task, chunked. You model one; they do one with a frame; they do one alone.
    12–17They explain back to you what they did, in a sentence. Echo and stretch.
    17–20One line in the log. Tell them one specific thing they did well.
