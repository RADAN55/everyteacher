The rules behind fair grading

What the law expects

  • English learners must have meaningful access to the same curriculum and the same grades as everyone else (Lau v. Nichols; the Equal Educational Opportunities Act). A grade that is really a language score denies that access twice: it hides the content the student learned and it penalizes the thing the school is supposed to be teaching.
  • The Castañeda standard asks whether the program actually works. Grades that track English level rather than content learning are the first evidence a reviewer reads that it doesn't.
  • Retention or failure for language reasons alone is not defensible. The student's Language Service Plan has a grading section for exactly this reason — read it.

What fair looks like in practice

  • Two scores, always. Content: did the student show the concept, in words, a diagram, a label, a list, aloud or in Spanish? Language: one feature, named, with feedback. The content score is the grade.
  • Same standard, different evidence. A Newcomer proves "describes the water cycle" with a labeled diagram and four words; a Challenge student with a paragraph. Both met the standard.
  • No courtesy pass. A C for "trying" tells the student and the family nothing and hides a gap. Score the content honestly; scaffold until the content shows.
  • Language counts where the standard is language. In ELA, the writing standards are the content. Even there, grade the feature you taught, not every error.
  • Tests are different. State tests are scored to their own rubrics; the fairness lever there is the accommodations in the LSP, in place before the window.
