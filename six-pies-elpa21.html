Listening scripts

Script A · Listen and match (3 items)
Say: I will describe a pie. Write the number of the pie.
A1. This pie is made with nuts. The top is brown and sticky because of the corn syrup.
A2. This pie has a white topping and a yellow filling. The topping is whipped cream, and there is lemon zest on top. The crust is made of graham crackers.
A3. This pie is orange. It is made with a vegetable, brown sugar and cinnamon.
Script B · Short conversation (3 items)
Say: Two students are planning a bake sale. Listen, then answer the questions.
Maya: We need two pies for the bake sale on Friday. I was thinking pecan pie — everybody loves it.
Daniel: Pecan is good, but pecans are expensive right now. Buttermilk pie costs almost nothing. It's just buttermilk, sugar, eggs and butter.
Maya: True. But people don't always know what buttermilk pie is. Pecan sells itself.
Daniel: Then let's do one of each. The pecan for the people who know what they want, and the buttermilk for the people who are curious.
Maya: Deal. I'll buy the pecans tonight. Can you make the buttermilk pie Thursday after school?
Daniel: Sure — it only takes an hour.
Script C · Short lecture (2 items)
Say: Listen to part of a cooking class.
Three of these pies — buttermilk, chess and sweet potato — are called custard pies. A custard is a mixture of eggs, sugar and milk or cream. When you bake a custard, the proteins in the eggs change shape and link together. That is what turns a liquid into a soft solid. If the oven is too hot, the proteins tighten too much, and the pie cracks or turns watery. So custard pies are usually baked at a moderate temperature, and the center should still move a little — the cooks say it should jiggle — when you take the pie out. It finishes setting as it cools.

Reading

Pies of the American South

In the American South, pie is more than a dessert. For many families it is a tradition that connects generations. Several of the most famous Southern pies were invented by cooks who had very little: no fresh fruit, no chocolate, and sometimes no refrigerator. They used what was in the kitchen — eggs, sugar, butter and milk — and created something new.

Buttermilk pie and chess pie are two examples. Both are simple custards. The difference is small: chess pie adds a spoonful of cornmeal, which gives the top a thin, crisp layer. Some people say "chess" comes from "chest," because the pie kept well in a pie chest, a wooden cabinet used before refrigerators. Others say a cook once called it "jes' pie" — just pie — and the name stuck.

Sweet potato pie has deep roots in African American cooking and is a center of the Thanksgiving table in many homes. Pecan pie uses the nut of a tree native to Mississippi and its neighbors; the sticky filling comes from corn syrup. Coconut cream pie and lemon icebox pie are newer. The icebox pie did not need an oven at all; it set in the icebox — the first home refrigerator — which made it popular in the hot summers of the 1930s.

Today you can find all six in diners from Texas to Virginia. The recipes have changed very little, which is exactly the point.

W4 · Opinion essay (Challenge) · also an ELPA21 "write an email" option

The cafeteria will serve one of these six pies at the Thanksgiving lunch. Which pie should it be? Write an essay of two or three paragraphs. State your opinion, give two reasons with details from the picture or the reading, and answer one argument against your choice. Or: write the same argument as an email to the cafeteria manager (greeting, purpose, reasons, polite closing).

Structure: In my opinion, the cafeteria should serve ___ because ___ and ___. · First, ___. For example, ___. · Second, ___. · Some people might say ___; however, ___. · For these reasons, ___.

TeacherLesson plan · one 90-minute block · grades 6–12 · EL block or sheltered content class

The 90-minute lesson: Six Southern pies

Content objectiveStudents will compare and contrast two Southern pies using details from an infographic and an informational text, and state and support an opinion about which one a school should serve.
Language objectivesListening: identify a described item and key details in a conversation and a short lecture. Reading: locate details, infer word meaning from context, and sequence a procedure. Speaking: describe a picture and give an opinion with reasons using because and first/then/finally. Writing: compare two items using both / only / unlike / however at the lane's length (frame sentences → paragraph → essay or email).
StandardsMS ELP 1, 2, 3, 4, 8, 9 (10 in feedback). ELPA21 task formats: Listen and Match, Short Conversation, Academic Lecture, Read for Details, Word Meaning, Sequence, Compare, Opinion, Observe and Report, Give Directions, Classroom Chat.
Key vocabularyFive words first: ingredient · crust · filling · bake · slice. Connectors: both, only, unlike, however, because, first/then/finally. Cognates on the wall: ingrediente, crema, canela, vainilla, limón, coco, tradicional, similar, diferente, receta.
MaterialsInfographic projected and on the student cover page · student packet by lane (print "Student pages") · teacher pages (scripts, key) · word bank and four frames on the board · a device per Progressing/Challenge pair for recording S4 · timer.
GroupingPairs, rotated: Newcomer with a partner who shows (not translates); Emerging with Progressing for the reading; same-lane pairs for writing. Teacher pulls the Newcomer/Emerging group during the writing block.

Differentiation at a glance

Newcomer · ELPT 1Listening A; bold-word matching; procedure with a partner; W1 labels + frames; S1 words, S3 with a showing partner; S2 with the teacher. Bilingual word list; pointing counts.
Emerging · ELPT 2Listening A + B (read 3×); reading 1, 2, 4 + procedure; W2 four frames; S1–S3 with frames on the board. Teacher-table during writing.
Progressing · ELPT 3–4All listening; all reading + evidence sentence; W3 paragraph with both/however/unlike; S1–S4, S4 recorded. Unpack the sentence with the class.
Challenge · ELPT 4–5 / native speakersAll of the above; reading extension (which "chess" story is more believable); W4 essay or email with a counterargument; S4 with a rebuttal.

Assessment

Formative, in the roomFinger checks after A and the reading; the underlined sentence; the W2/W3 connector check at minute 70; the exit ticket sorted by lane.
Products to scoreListening + reading items (key on the teacher page); the writing task for the lane; the S4 recording (Progressing/Challenge) or the teacher's S2 note (Newcomer/Emerging). Score content fully; one language pattern.
Evidence for the LSPEach product maps to a domain: a dated W2/W3 sample for the writing goal, the S4 recording for speaking, item scores for listening and reading. File one per student.

If you have 45 minutes instead

Day 1: open, five words, cognate hunt, Listening A–B, Reading (0–45). Day 2: unpack the sentence, S1 + S3, writing by lane, exit ticket. Save S4 for a warm-up on day 3.

Extension and home connection

Students bring (or draw) a dessert from home and fill in the same six-line ingredient card as the infographic, in English and the home language. Next class, the class infographic becomes the next task set. Content tie-ins: FACS/Culinary (the procedure), Chemistry (Script C: protein denaturation), U.S. History (foodways, the icebox).

Scoring notes (in the spirit of the ELPA21 rubrics)

WritingEmerging (W1–W2): credit a correct idea in a frame even with spelling or verb errors; a label or list counts. Progressing (W3): look for the three connectors used correctly, three ingredients named, and sentences that compare rather than list. Challenge (W4): a stated opinion, two supported reasons, a counterargument addressed, paragraph structure, and the email conventions if that option is chosen. Grade the content fully; give language feedback on one pattern.
SpeakingScore what was communicated first: task completed? Then language: sentence-level control, vocabulary from the set, connectors (because, first/then), and for S4 the counterargument. Pronunciation counts only if it blocks meaning. Let Newcomers point and name; that is the task at their level.
