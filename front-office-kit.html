The front office kit.

The family at the counter met you before they met any teacher. This is everything the office needs to enroll a child who speaks another language — today, legally, kindly — in the order you will need it.

Free · no account · nothing you type leaves this browser

The first ten minutes

A family is at the counter and you do not share a language. This is the whole procedure. Print it and tape it under the counter.

1 · Find the language

Point to the board

Turn the I speak… board toward the family. They point to their language. Say the language name back and smile. You now know what to ask for.

2 · Get a voice

Call the interpreter line

Your district's phone or video interpreter number goes here: Family members, older siblings and the student are not the interpreter for enrollment, discipline or health. A bilingual staff member may help with directions and small talk while you wait.

3 · Enroll today

Start the paperwork now

Enrollment is not delayed for missing records, a missing Social Security number or any question of immigration status — none of those may be asked. Take what the family has, list what is missing, and give them a date to bring it.

The three things the office owns

The Home Language Survey

Every new student, every time, in a language the family understands. Any answer other than English sends the form to the EL teacher the same day. It is about language, never about status — say so if a family hesitates.

The clock

The screener and the parent notice run on a deadline from the day the student enrolls: thirty calendar days from the first day of school for students who start with the year, two weeks (many Mississippi plans say ten school days) for anyone who enrolls later. The office starts the clock; the EL teacher finishes it.

Language access

Enrollment papers, the handbook, discipline, health, grades and meetings reach the family in a language they understand, through a competent interpreter or translation, free. That is federal law (Title VI), not a courtesy. Log the language in the student system so every teacher sees it.

Do · don't

Do
  • Enroll the same day with whatever documents exist.
  • Accept any proof of age and address the district accepts from anyone — a lease, a utility bill, an affidavit of residence.
  • Give the Home Language Survey in the family's language.
  • Write the family's language and preferred contact in the student system.
  • Hand the family the welcome guide in their language and the school's hours, lunch form and bus form.
  • Tell the EL teacher and the counselor today.
Don't
  • Ask for a Social Security number as a condition of enrollment, or anything about visas, status or how the family entered the country.
  • Send a family away to "come back with papers."
  • Use a child, sibling or Google Translate for enrollment, health, discipline or special-education conversations.
  • Change or "Americanize" a name — enter it as the family writes it, in order.
  • Guess a birth date format: ask which number is the day and which the month.
Immunizations. Mississippi requires an immunization certificate (Form 121) for school entry. Start the enrollment, send the family to the school nurse or county health department with a written note of what is needed, and set the start date with the nurse — do not turn the family away without that plan.

What happens to the answers

  • Any language other than English on questions 1–3 → the student is screened for English proficiency (the ELPT screener) within the window. The survey does not make a student an EL; the screener does.
  • Question 4 is the family's language for everything the school sends home. Enter it in the student system today.
  • The survey is kept in the cumulative folder. It is the first document a federal review asks for.
  • Tell the family in plain words: This asks what language you speak so we can help your child and talk with you. It has nothing to do with immigration.

The rules in one breath
  • Free to the family, always.
  • Competent: someone trained to interpret, who knows school words, and who keeps it confidential. Being bilingual is not the same thing.
  • Not the child, not a sibling, not a classmate for enrollment, health, discipline, special education or anything a parent must decide.
  • Machine translation (apps, Google Translate) is fine for a hallway hello and a lunch-menu question, not for anything the family signs or decides.
  • Written notices — the handbook, discipline, grades, the EL letters — go home translated or with an interpreter's explanation.
Title VI of the Civil Rights Act; ESSA §1112(e)(4); 2015 USDOJ/OCR Dear Colleague Letter.
