What's new in teaching English learners: eight ideas shaping 2026

A sourced research brief — instructional frameworks, newcomer and SLIFE practice, AI, and Mississippi policy — with every concept tagged by how strong its evidence is.

The 2025–2026 EL/multilingual-learner field is defined less by a single new pedagogy than by three converging shifts: a settled "yes AND" consensus that Science of Reading foundational skills must be paired with oral language, vocabulary, content knowledge and explicit cross-linguistic transfer; a wave of implementation tooling (WIDA Key Language Uses, SLIFE guidance, standards-aligned AI prompting) that packages existing theory for classroom use; and a policy environment that simultaneously withdrew federal technical assistance (OELA cut to one staffer, the 2015 Dear Colleague Letter rescinded) while raising the stakes of state accountability for EL growth. The best-evidenced instructional pillars for a 9–12 EL program remain explicit foundational skills where needed, daily structured oral language, contrastive L1–English instruction, knowledge-rich content reading with embedded language work, and co-taught access to grade-level courses (Education Week, Sept 2025; IES K.L.I. project). The loudest new concepts, AI translation and AI-leveled texts, have the thinnest evidence and the sharpest expert cautions (EdSurge, Nov 2025). For Mississippi specifically, the operative 2025–26 facts are an all-four-domains Level 4 ELPT exit rule, a criterion-referenced accountability baseline year in which EL "Progress to Proficiency" carries 50 of 1,000 high-school points, and a state AI framework that never mentions English learners (MPSD EL Plan 2025–26; MDE accountability overview; DeSoto County News, May 2026). A major honesty caveat runs through this report: almost every published exemplar of the "hot" instructional concepts is K–5 and dual-language; peer-reviewed secondary-specific evidence from 2024–2026 was not located in the underlying research, and several Mississippi primary documents were unreachable.

"Science of Reading, yes AND" becomes the organizing instructional frame

The single most-covered instructional story of 2025 was the reconciliation of Science of Reading (SoR) policy with English-learner needs. Education Week's September 2025 special report found that ELs "often receive inadequate attention in 'science of reading' policy implementation," and that the National Committee for Effective Literacy (NCEL) and the Reading League now agree ELs need "intensive instruction in developing spoken English skills" AND "phonics and phonemic awareness," framed explicitly as "not an either or question" (Education Week, Sept 2025). The scale of the gap is quantified: a Shanker Institute analysis found only 10 of 45+ states with SoR mandates specifically address EL needs through targeted training or coaches, and a RAND June 2025 survey found roughly half of teachers of ELs feel "not at all" or "only somewhat" prepared to teach them (Education Week, Sept 2025). The evidence tier here is the strongest in the field: a consensus statement from two national organizations grounded in decades of bilingual-literacy research, now moving into district implementation case studies. Education Week's year-end list ranked the SoR-with-MLs implementation story fifth among the ten stories that defined 2025 (Education Week, Dec 2025).

The practice that falls out of this consensus is cross-linguistic transfer, or "teach what doesn't transfer." Southside ISD in San Antonio keeps "running lists of non-transferable English literacy skills," teaches explicitly how letters such as "A" function differently across languages, and leverages Latin and Greek cognates in grades 4–5 morphology; as one leader put it, "There's no need to reteach the whole alphabet...we [only] want to teach what doesn't transfer" (Education Week, Oct 2025). Wisconsin's CESA 3 built a UFLI companion guide highlighting Spanish–English connections (Education Week, Sept 2025). The principle is research-backed; the specific district routines are emerging. In a secondary EL block this looks like a posted Spanish–English "transfers / doesn't transfer" anchor chart (cognates such as información on one side; short vowels, /z/, final consonant clusters on the other) used during decoding and spelling of content vocabulary. The honest limitation: the Southside article contains no secondary-level content, and no 2024–2026 source on structured literacy for adolescent newcomers with interrupted schooling surfaced, so secondary adaptation is extrapolation rather than documented practice.

Two further framework developments sit on top of the SoR consensus. First, WIDA's strategy for 2025–27 is depth of implementation rather than a new standards edition: the 2025–26 release added an ELD Standards Framework Implementation Guide, Key Language Uses posters (Narrate, Inform, Explain, Argue), a Marco DALE Spanish framework explorer, a UDL-and-language planning reflection tool, and new "Reading With Multilingual Learners" and "Multilingual Learners With Disabilities" pages; the 2026–27 release added a research handout on "Actionable approaches for centering newcomers" and a Focus Bulletin on language in reading assessment (WIDA 2025–26; WIDA 2026–27). This is policy-driven guidance, not effect research, but it is what consortium-state PD (Alabama, Georgia, Tennessee) will be framed around; a 10th-grade biology unit tagged "Explain," with a WIDA Language Expectation beside the content objective and cause–effect connectors modeled from the Language Charts, is the intended classroom form. Second, Corwin's June 2025 Academic Languaging (Ernst-Slavit and Gottlieb) reframes academic language from a static register to teach into "an active, collaborative student-driven process," arguing that vocabulary-list and sentence-formula approaches "underutilized students' linguistic and cultural strengths" (Sage/Corwin). It signals a mainstream-publisher shift from "teach the academic register" toward "leverage the repertoire to do academic work," and its classroom form is students co-constructing a science explanation multimodally (talk, sketch, home-language notes) before producing a written claim rather than completing a teacher-supplied frame. It is an emerging practitioner-scholar framework, not an outcome study.

The knowledge-building strand supplies the most rigorously tested curriculum model, with a caveat. The IES-funded K.L.I. (Knowledge, Language, and Structured Inquiry) program, developed 2020–2025 at NC State for grades 3–5, organizes 41 small-group lessons around five repeating components (Discovery Reading, Confident Reading, Breaking Words, Sentence Workshop, Uncover the Structure). It produced significant positive effects on content-knowledge assessments but only positive, non-significant effects on standardized reading comprehension, with comprehension improving indirectly through English proficiency, content knowledge and fluency (IES). That pattern, strong proximal gains and weak distal gains, mirrors the broader knowledge-building literature and is the caveat to carry into any "knowledge-rich curricula fix ML comprehension" claim. Adapted to a 9–12 block, the structure is a two-week topic cycle (for example, "How do cities get water?") with a Discovery Read, a morphology mini-lesson (aqua-, -tion), one dense sentence unpacked in a Sentence Workshop, a text-structure map, and a short written explanation. No secondary RCT of this model exists.

Secondary structures: co-teaching, time-boxed newcomer programs, and the LTEL early flag

The most secondary-specific research infrastructure of the period is federal: IES invested $20 million in two centers on secondary ELs, the WestEd-led National R&D Center to Improve Education for Secondary English Learners and the Center for the Success of English Learners (CSEL) at UT Austin's Meadows Center (IES; Meadows Center). The WestEd center targets long-term ELs and two barriers, restricted access to rigorous coursework and insufficient quality learning opportunities, and its co-PIs Amanda Kibler and Martha Palacios presented preliminary findings from a national survey of co-teaching and collaboration between content and ELD teachers in secondary schools, noting these models "have become increasingly common" but that their national prevalence "is not known" (WestEd, Feb 2024). Co-teaching is therefore best classed as emerging-to-research-backed: a large practitioner literature with survey evidence forming. In practice it is a 10th-grade English block where the ELA teacher leads a close read while the ELD co-teacher runs a parallel station on the same text with a sentence-deconstruction routine, both working from one language objective written from the WIDA "Argue" Key Language Use.

Newcomer and SLIFE programming is where 2025 produced the most actionable new guidance, against a backdrop of collapsing enrollment. The 2023–24 newcomer surge reversed sharply: Miami-Dade enrolled roughly 2,550 new-to-country students in 2025–26 versus nearly 14,000 the prior year, Houston ISD closed Las Americas Newcomer School after enrollment fell from 111 to 21, and Albertville City Schools in Alabama reported its newcomer academy enrolling zero new students, with the superintendent expecting to lose 12 teacher positions (Education Week, Nov 2025). Albertville is the closest documented Southeast analog to a small Mississippi district, and the lesson is that programs built as separate centers are fragile; a within-school strand that can scale down is more durable. On program design, the evidence favors a hybrid. Montgomery County (MD) Public Schools' December 2025 evaluation of secondary SLIFE programs found them "largely implemented as designed" with multidisciplinary teams, and students grew in English proficiency, literacy, math and credit accumulation, but most had not reached grade-level benchmarks and required continued support after exiting; the district recommended research-based curricula designed for older students with interrupted schooling and a permanent interrupted-schooling indicator in the student information system (MCPS, Dec 2025). Nashville's Newcomer Academy evolved from a sheltered classroom into a partially integrated model with a one-year typical stay, co-teaching with certified ESL teachers, a multilingual counselor and SEL advisories, reporting 1.5–2 years of ACCESS growth per year (Educating All Learners Alliance). WIDA's October 2025 Focus Bulletin cites Metro Nashville's SIFE Transition Facilitator model, under which served students earned 15.5 credits versus 3 for comparison students with fewer absences and referrals (WIDA Focus Bulletin, Oct 2025). The emerging consensus design is a time-boxed sheltered core, partial integration, a named transition facilitator tracking credits and attendance, and a hard exit goal, followed by a continuum into sheltered content and co-taught mainstream rather than a stand-alone academy.

The same WIDA bulletin is the most current authoritative SLIFE guidance. It defines SLIFE as students missing two or more years of schooling, estimates them at 10–20% of identified multilingual learners, and prescribes five practices: foundational literacy "with letters, sounds, and language tied to meaningful and local community landmarks"; trauma-informed approaches; a responsive classroom with mood meters, journals and arts integration for sharing personal stories; explicit instruction in the "culture of schooling" (procedures, technology, behavioral expectations peers take for granted); and integrated language and content (WIDA Focus Bulletin, Oct 2025). Inside a 90-minute block, a defensible SLIFE/Newcomer lane is roughly 20–25 minutes of decoding and encoding anchored in local content (a "Laurel landmarks" decodable set and labeled photo walks), a 10-minute "how school works" routine, and the remainder in integrated content with a mood-meter check-in. Note that the bulletin's own research base for program characteristics is still Short and Boyson (2004) and CCSSO (2018); the 2024–26 literature on program models is thin, and no 2024–26 update to MALP (DeCapua and Marshall) or CUNY's Bridges program was verified.

Long-term EL policy is moving toward earlier flags, with California as bellwether. In May 2026 Governor Newsom's trailer bill proposed a single statewide LTEL definition (no proficiency after seven years) and a narrower at-risk definition at six years; EL advocates countered with a flag at four years at ELPAC Level 2 or below, or five to six years at Level 3 or below, and an Assembly subcommittee rejected the governor's version (EdSource, May 2026). The research underneath is older but large: in a WIDA sample of 813,155 students tracked 2006–2019, ever-IEP ELs were about four times more likely to become LTELs (55% versus 14%) and plateaued around a composite 4.0 (WIDA Research Report, 2021). For an ELPA21 state, the analogous practice is an internal at-risk flag at year four for students stuck at Level 2–3, beginning with a dual-identification review of whether the plateau is language, disability, or both. Mississippi has no statutory LTEL definition that the research located, and no 2025–26 Southern state LTEL policy source was retrieved.

AI enters EL classrooms faster than guidance or evidence

Adoption is outrunning both policy and research. An EdWeek Research Center survey (fall 2024, N=1,135) found 33% of administrators already using AI in multilingual programs and 40% considering or piloting (Education Week, Dec 2024), and Chalkbeat reported in August 2025 that teachers were leaning on chatbots (Brisk), reading assistants (Amira) and translation while the administration pushed AI integration and eliminated nearly all OELA staff (Chalkbeat, Aug 2025). Guidance is bifurcated: the professional language bodies speak directly to ELs while general K–12 AI policy is EL-blind. TESOL's Board approved a "Position Statement: AI for the ELT Profession" in October 2025 supporting "responsible and ethical integration" while insisting "human expertise remains irreplaceable" and that educators must "recognize and mitigate" algorithmic bias (TESOL). WIDA's "Aligning AI for Multilingual Learners" webinar and packet instruct teachers to embed the ELD Standards Framework in prompts so output is differentiated by design rather than generic (WIDA). Meanwhile, of 40 states with K–12 AI guidance, only Oregon and Massachusetts explicitly mention multilingual learners, and Mississippi's May 2026 Statewide AI Framework, organized around 11 skill domains with "high-stakes human-review triggers" for grading decisions, contains no mention of English learners at all (Playlab tracker; DeSoto County News, May 2026). A Mississippi EL specialist therefore has a state framework to cite but no state language on EL-specific use; TESOL and WIDA become the de facto references.

The practice concepts cluster into two camps with opposite risk profiles. Production-oriented uses keep students generating English. The only controlled evidence of benefit sits here: a randomized field experiment (N=363, non-K–12) found unrestricted AI conversation partners improved lexical diversity by 5.90% overall and 9.53% for below-average-proficiency learners while lowering evaluation anxiety (HICSS 2025). The IES-funded U-GAIN Reading center ($10M, Digital Promise with Amira, Penn and MDRC, 2024–2029) is building the K–12 causal evidence, but has published no outcomes yet (Digital Promise). Access-oriented uses (translation, leveling) reduce barriers but carry the avoidance and over-simplification risks that dominate 2025–26 practitioner reporting. Sarah Carr, an LIEP department chair in Virginia, describes students using AI as a "life vest," submitting work "suspiciously polished" for their proficiency, and responds by grounding writing in oral production so students must "defend written work verbally"; Lisa Egle requires video documentation of which AI revisions students accepted or rejected and why (Education Week, May 2026). The avoidance problem is consistently reported but not quantified in any study.

Two research findings should shape any EL program's AI rules. First, generic AI simplification strips the vocabulary learners need: Li, Arase and Crespi (NAACL 2025) show off-the-shelf "make this easier" output under-exposes learners to target-level words, and their CEFR-aligned method raised target-level vocabulary coverage by more than 20% (arXiv); Chalkbeat separately reports the equity worry that easier materials "amplify inequitable access to grade-level content" (Chalkbeat, Aug 2025). AI-leveled text sets are therefore a legitimate tool only when the teacher checks that target vocabulary survives the leveling. Second, AI detectors discriminate against ELs: a Stanford study found a 61.3% false-positive rate on non-native TOEFL essays versus under 10% for native essays, and co-author James Zou advised "more in-person discussions and assessments" (Tech & Learning). Related UW-Madison work found multimodal LLMs perform poorly on Spanglish, so AI assessment of translanguaged work may "underestimate multilingual student knowledge" (UW-Madison IDS). Academic-integrity decisions based on detectors risk disparate impact on ELs; process-based assessment (revision logs, oral defense) is the defensible alternative. Live translation hardware (Pocketalk, Pear Deck translation, Duoecho glasses) is in mainstream classrooms with reported participation gains, but experts frame it as "one type of support" among many, note it "struggles with children's voices," and warn students "may struggle without them in subsequent grades" (EdSurge, Nov 2025). No source describes a district protocol governing teacher use of in-platform translation, and no K–12 outcome study on translation or captioning exists. A sound 9th-grade routine is AI for input access (a newcomer follows a world-history mini-lecture via device), then structured English output (a bilingual organizer and one sentence reported to a partner).

Federal retreat and a Mississippi baseline year turn policy mechanics into teaching concepts

The 2025–26 policy shocks did not change the law, but they changed who supplies the "how." Starting January 2025 the Education Department laid off all but one OELA staff member, and on February 13, 2026 it gave Congress 90-day notice of a plan to dissolve the office, with Title III grant administration already moved to the Department of Labor in November 2025 (Education Week, Apr 2026; Learning Forward, Nov 2025). The $890 million Title III-A program was withheld for roughly three weeks in July 2025 before release after a lawsuit (K-12 Dive, Jul 2025). In August 2025 ED and DOJ rescinded the 2015 EL Dear Colleague Letter; rescinding guidance does not change underlying law, and EL rights still stem from Supreme Court precedent and national-origin statutes (Chalkbeat, Aug 2025). New America's Leslie Villegas summarized the net effect: "legal obligations to these students have not changed. What has changed is their ability to ask the federal government for help" (Education Week, Apr 2026). Districts can no longer cite the 2015 letter as the operational manual, so state EL guidelines and local LSP/SET documentation become the controlling documents, and Title III-funded PD and materials become higher-risk line items. Whether OELA was actually dissolved after the May 2026 notice window is unverified.

Mississippi's own 2025–26 changes convert accountability mechanics into instructional concepts. On June 19, 2025 the State Board set the ELPT (ELPA21) exit standard at Level 4 or higher in every domain and added an Alt ELPA option (CitizenPortal summary); district plans operationalize this with four years of post-exit monitoring reviewed every nine weeks by the SET and LSPs updated annually (MPSD EL Plan 2025–26). On November 20, 2025 the State Board moved the A–F system from norm-referenced to criterion-referenced scoring with higher cuts (A = 769 of 1,000 for high schools), making 2025–26 a non-comparable baseline year (Mississippi Free Press), and MDE's overview lists EL "Progress to Proficiency" at 50 of 1,000 high-school points (MDE). Three derived concepts follow, and the labels are syntheses rather than quotations from any source: "domain-gap targeting" (a student at 5/5/5/3 does not exit, so instruction concentrates on the lowest domain, usually Writing or Speaking at secondary); "growth-to-proficiency pacing" (per-student, per-domain ELPT level targets set in fall and checked on the existing nine-week SET cadence, because each student's growth now has visible school-grade value measured against fixed targets, not peers); and "monitoring-year supports" (the four-year window as a structured obligation). Louisiana's new system offers a comparison point, excluding newly arrived ELs from proficiency for two years but including them in ELPT growth from year two (LDOE FAQ), and Texas drops the parental-approval requirement for reclassification from 2026–27 (TEA), a signal that readiness evidence rather than sign-off will increasingly drive exit. For WIDA-state neighbors, 2025–26 ACCESS scores are a "new baseline" not comparable to prior years, with new cut scores effective spring 2027 after the July 2026 standard setting (WIDA). Unverified Mississippi items: MDE's business rule for what counts as EL "progress," the EL-specific changes in the February 2026 MTAM edition, ELPA21's shortened summative details, and any 2025–26 reissue of MDE's EL Guidelines (mdek12.org and elpa21.org PDFs returned 403 errors).

Immigration enforcement produced the year's most measurable non-instructional effect and a new, mostly procedural, "belonging" toolkit. EdWeek Research Center found 39% of educators citing enforcement-related absences in spring 2026, up from 24% in fall 2025, with 57% reporting student anxiety and 72% of schools holding protocols for federal agents requesting access (Education Week, Jun 2026). In Chicago, EL attendance fell 1.3 points after a September 2025 operation, and documented responses were excused "well-being" absences, parent "walking trains," staff presence at arrival and dismissal, and Know-Your-Rights distribution (Chalkbeat Chicago, Oct 2025). A South Carolina educator survey found 78% of EL teachers saw changes in MLs' emotional well-being (Education Week, May 2026), and Tennessee's HB 836, passed by the House in March 2026, would require schools to verify and report students' immigration status, a bill explicitly designed to generate a Plyler test case (Chalkbeat TN, Mar 2026). The documented ~2-point absence effect for foreign-born students is large enough to threaten ELPT participation thresholds in a baseline accountability year. Enforcement-aware belonging is therefore an emerging, descriptive concept: trusted-messenger communication, a bilingual liaison calling within 24 hours of an absence, home-language rights materials at intake, and enrollment protocols that never ask immigration status. Academic-belonging practices (identity texts, bilingual buddies) remain guidance-endorsed and qualitatively studied, with no 2024–26 US high school outcome evidence. The one measurable asset-based lever available in Mississippi is the Seal of Biliteracy: nationally, ~40% of the 158,384 recipients in 2022–23 were current or former ELs, Mississippi adopted the Seal in 2019 with LEA opt-in and a requirement to report former-EL recipients separately, but no statewide recipient count or confirmation of Laurel City School District participation was found (Education Week, Dec 2024; Language Policy blog).

The gaps are as informative as the findings. No peer-reviewed 2024–2026 article from TESOL Quarterly, Reading Research Quarterly or similar journals on secondary ELs was located within the research budget; no updated WWC practice guide for ELs exists beyond the 2014 elementary-and-middle guide (WWC); no new Zwiers, Kinsella, Olsen, MALP or Cummins publication was verified; "comprehensible input resurgence," "oral language first," "writing-to-learn for ELs" and "same text, different scaffold" surfaced in no dated source and should not be presented as named 2025–26 trends; conference themes for TESOL 2026, NABE 2026 and La Cosecha were not retrieved (the NABE page fetched was the economists' association); and Mississippi-specific newcomer counts, Seal recipient data, EL-progress business rules and MTAM changes were all unreachable. The phrases "teacher-in-the-loop," "AI-leveled text sets," "growth-to-proficiency pacing" and "reclassification readiness" are labels assigned to practices the sources describe, not terms the sources use.

Conclusion

The field's center of gravity in 2025–26 is implementation, not invention: the big ideas (comprehensive literacy with transfer, knowledge-rich content reading with embedded language, co-taught access, translanguaging-informed academic work) were settled in principle by 2023, and the past two years have been about packaging them into guides, bulletins, prompts and district routines. That makes the honest headline for an experienced practitioner uncomfortable but useful: the concepts with the strongest evidence are the least novel, almost none have been tested at the high-school level, and the genuinely new entrants (AI translation, leveling, conversation partners) are being adopted on practitioner faith while the first rigorous K–12 study is still years from results. A defensible secondary program in 2026 therefore borrows its instructional core from elementary-validated principles adapted upward, borrows its structures from a handful of district evaluations (MCPS, Nashville), and treats AI through a single filter that the evidence does support: access tools must be followed by a required English-production step, and assessment must move from detection to process.

The second implication is that policy mechanics are now pedagogy. With federal technical assistance gone, an all-domains exit rule, a criterion-referenced baseline year, and enforcement-driven attendance volatility, the Mississippi EL teacher's most consequential "new concepts" are not in any book: target the weakest ELPT domain per student, pace growth against fixed targets on the nine-week SET cadence, keep SLIFE and newcomer flags portable because students leave mid-year, and build belonging through procedures that families can see. Those practices are also the ones a resource-publishing specialist can document and share most credibly, because the evidence for them lives in state rules and district data rather than in a research base that has not yet caught up to grades 9–12.
