How a second language is actually acquired

Five findings from second language acquisition research, and what each one asks you to do differently on Monday.

Most of what slows an English learner down in a content classroom is not ability, and it is not effort. It is a mismatch between how we teach language and how language is learned. These five findings have held up across decades of research, and every one of them has a move attached.

1

Language is acquired through understanding, not repetition

Students acquire English when they understand messages slightly beyond their current level — what Krashen called comprehensible input. Drills and word lists produce recall; understood language produces acquisition.

Your move: keep the content the same and make the message reachable — visuals, gestures, examples, a slower rate with normal vocabulary. Never lower the concept to lower the language.

2

Silence is a stage, not refusal

Newcomers often go through a silent period, absorbing far more than they produce. A student who won't speak in week three may be building the system they'll use in week twelve.

Your move: let them show understanding without talking — point, sort, match, draw, thumbs up. Invite speech; don't force it in front of thirty peers.

3

Anxiety blocks acquisition

The affective filter is real: when students are embarrassed or afraid of being wrong, input stops getting through. Cold calls and public error correction raise that filter fastest.

Your move: rehearse answers in pairs before anyone speaks publicly, and respond to meaning first. Learn to pronounce their name correctly — it matters more than any strategy on this page.

4

Output is where the gaps show up

Swain's work on pushed output holds that producing language forces students to notice what they can't yet say. Comprehension alone will not carry a student to Level 4.

Your move: every period, require one extended spoken or written response with a frame to support it. A comprehension check is not output.

5

What they know in Spanish transfers to English

Cummins' work on transfer holds that concepts and literacy skills cross languages — the idea does not have to be relearned, only relabeled.

Your move: let students think, discuss, and pre-write in Spanish on the way to English, and point out cognates — analyze / analizar, evidence / evidencia, hypothesis / hipótesis.

1–3 vs. 5–7 Years to fluency

Conversational ease arrives first; the academic language of analysis and argument takes far longer. Social fluency is not academic readiness.

1 → 7 Seconds of wait time

Average wait time is about one second. An English learner needs roughly seven to hear, process, form, and check an answer.

SIOP in eight moves

The Sheltered Instruction Observation Protocol (Echevarría, Vogt, and Short) organizes sheltered teaching into eight components. Here is one thing each asks of a content classroom.

1

Lesson preparation

Post a content objective and a language objective for every lesson, and say both out loud. Students should be able to tell a visitor what they're learning and how they'll use language to show it.

2

Building background

Connect explicitly to what students already know — including schooling from another country — and pre-teach the three key terms the lesson depends on.

3

Comprehensible input

Match your rate to proficiency, define academic terms in the moment, and demonstrate every task before assigning it. Model, then release.

4

Strategies

Teach students how to learn: previewing, chunking, note-taking frames, self-questioning. Scaffold with think-alouds and questions that ask for reasoning, not recall.

5

Interaction

Structure frequent talk about content in pairs and triads, with grouping that changes by task. Aim to talk less than your students do, and allow home-language clarification.

6

Practice and application

Give students something to handle, build, sort, or produce that uses all four domains. New vocabulary sticks when it's used, not when it's copied.

7

Lesson delivery

Keep both objectives visibly supported all period, hold engagement high, and pace to the students in front of you rather than the plan on paper.

8

Review and assessment

Close by reviewing key vocabulary and concepts, give feedback on language and content separately, and check comprehension of all students before they leave.

Keep the thinking hard and the language reachable. Scaffolding is not lowering the bar — it's building the stairs.

Writing the two objectives

Our walkthrough expectation is a posted MS ELA standard, the MS ELP/ELPA21 standard, a content objective, a language objective, and can-do statements. The two objectives are where most of us stall.

The content objective names what students will know or be able to do. The language objective names the English they'll use to get there — a domain, a function, and a support.

Biology

Content I can explain how enzymes affect reaction rate.

Language I can orally explain a cause-and-effect relationship using because, therefore, and as a result.

Algebra I

Content I can solve two-step equations.

Language I can write a justification of each step using the frame "First I ______ because ______."

U.S. History

Content I can identify causes of the Great Migration.

Language I can discuss causes with a partner using a word bank of push and pull factors.

Health Sciences

Content I can demonstrate proper hand hygiene.

Language I can read and sequence the procedural steps using ordinal transitions.

Clip and keep

Three moves that cost nothing

Count to seven

Silent counting to seven after you ask a question changes who raises a hand. It costs you a few seconds a day.

Recast instead of correct

"Yesterday he go to the lab" becomes "Right — yesterday he went to the lab. What happened next?" The correction lands, the conversation continues, and the filter stays down.

Leave the anchor up

The chart you built Tuesday is a lifeline Thursday. Keep word banks, visuals, and worked examples posted for the whole unit instead of erasing at the bell.

Research corner

Four findings from second language acquisition research that hold up across studies, and what each one changes about a Tuesday.

Thomas & Collier Time to academic English

Four to seven years, even in strong programs

Long-term studies of academic achievement consistently put full academic English proficiency several years out, well past the point where a student sounds fluent. Program quality shortens the timeline; nothing eliminates it.

In your room: judge growth against where the student started, not against a native-speaking peer, and expect scaffolding to be necessary long after conversation is easy.

Nation Lexical coverage

Comprehension needs about 95 to 98 percent known words

Below that threshold, unknown vocabulary crowds out meaning and students cannot guess their way through a passage. Words also need many encounters in context before they stick — one exposure on a list is not one of them.

In your room: pre-teach the few words that carry the passage, then recycle them deliberately in your talk, your questions, and the exit ticket.

Long, Gass Interaction and negotiation

Acquisition accelerates when meaning gets negotiated

Input matters most when learners have to work at it with someone — clarifying, confirming, rephrasing. That back-and-forth makes language comprehensible and simultaneously pushes production.

In your room: build in structured pair tasks where partners must ask each other something to finish the work, rather than round-robin answers to you.

Lyster & Ranta Corrective feedback

Recasts feel kind, prompts get repair

Their classroom analysis found recasts to be the most common form of feedback and among the least likely to be noticed as correction, while prompts — clarification requests, elicitation, repetition of the error point — produced far more student self-repair.

In your room: keep recasting for flow, but when accuracy matters, pause and elicit: "Say that again with the past tense." Let the student make the fix.

Compliance corner

The part that shows up in a file review.

  • The LSP is a legal document. Every EL has a Language Service Plan listing required supports. Under the EEOA and the Castañeda standard, those supports have to happen in the general education classroom — not just in the EL block.
  • Accommodations change access, not the standard. Extended time, translated directions, and a word-to-word dictionary change how a student reaches the content. Shortening the assignment lowers the standard, and that is a modification.
  • Test accommodations must be everyday accommodations. Under the Mississippi Testing Accommodations Manual, a support may be used on a state assessment only if the student already uses it in daily instruction. Start it now, not in the spring.
  • Parents get information in a language they understand. Grades, discipline, and program notices require an interpreter or a translated notice — not a bilingual student or sibling.
  • Document it. A line in your plans or grade book — support used, date, student response — is what turns "we do that" into evidence.

Ask the EL office

My newcomer hasn't said a word in six weeks. Should I be worried?

Usually not. A silent period of several weeks to several months is a normal stage of acquisition, and receptive language is developing the whole time. What matters is whether the student is engaging non-verbally — following directions, tracking the lesson, completing sorts and matching tasks, working with a partner. If those are happening, keep the input comprehensible and give low-risk ways to respond. If a student is disengaged in every domain, or you're seeing gaps that don't look like language, come see me before starting a referral.
