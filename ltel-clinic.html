The long-term EL clinic.

Six years an English learner, fluent in the hallway, stuck at Writing 2 or 3 every spring. They are the largest group on most rosters and the most invisible. Twelve weeks on the academic register — one feature a week, on their own content reading — is the map out.

Who they are

A long-term English learner has been in U.S. schools six years or more and is still an EL. Most sound fluent, many were born here, and almost all are stuck in the same place: Listening and Speaking at 4 or 5, Reading and Writing at 2 or 3, year after year. They are the largest group in most Mississippi high schools' EL counts, the hardest to see, and the ones the accountability model counts most heavily.

What it looks like

  • Oral English indistinguishable from classmates; academic reading and writing two or three grade levels behind.
  • Writes the way they talk: short sentences, missing endings, thing and a lot, no transitions.
  • Passes by doing the minimum quietly; rarely called on; rarely asks.
  • Often literate in neither language at an academic level — and told for years that they are "almost there."

How it happened

  • Services that changed every time they changed schools, or stopped.
  • Social English arrived in two years and was mistaken for the whole job.
  • Nobody ever taught the academic register explicitly — it was assumed to come with time.
  • Lowered expectations that felt like kindness: shorter tasks, no writing, no feedback.

Why it matters now

  • Mississippi's accountability model credits EL progress on the ELPT; a student who scores the same level six years running pulls the school's indicator down every year.
  • Exit is 4 or higher in every domain; for an LTEL the gap is almost always Writing, then Reading — teachable, specific, finite.
  • They are on the graduation clock. English II and the ACT reward exactly the language this clinic teaches.
The frame to hold. The student is not behind; the student is stuck in one place, and that place has a map. Twelve weeks of explicit, daily work on the academic register — in the EL block and echoed in content classes — moves Writing a level more reliably than anything else we have.

The thirty-minute diagnostic

Before week 1. Four parts, one sitting, scored on the spot. It tells you which of the four clinic profiles the student fits and gives the student a baseline they will beat.

10 min

1 · Writing sample

Prompt from a current content class: Explain one cause of ___ and its effect. Ten minutes, no help. Score the four features below (0–3 each): sentence variety (any subordination or relative clauses?), academic vocabulary (content words, nominalizations), verb control (tense consistent, endings present), organization (claim → support → so-what, with transitions).

8 min

2 · Reading check

One grade-level paragraph from the same class, read aloud by the student (note hesitations on multisyllable academic words), then three questions: one literal, one inference, one vocabulary-in-context. Score 0–3.

6 min

3 · Academic vocabulary

Twenty words from the general academic list (analyze, evidence, significant, contrast, however, consequently, process, structure, interpret, assume…). Student says a sentence for each. Count the ones used correctly.

6 min

4 · The interview

Three questions: How long have you been an EL? What do you think is keeping you from exiting? What do you want to be able to do in English that you can't yet? Write the answers down verbatim; read them back at week 12.

The twelve weeks

One language feature a week, taught explicitly, practiced on this week's content reading from the student's own classes, checked on Friday. The daily rhythm in the EL block: Mon teach the feature on a mentor sentence · Tue find it in the content text · Wed write with it · Thu talk with it (structured academic talk) · Fri exit check and a two-minute goal conference.

Week 1

Sentence combining

Teach: Take two or three short sentences and make one, with a subordinator or a relative clause.

Do: Mentor: Because the colonists had no representative, they refused the tax. Students combine six pairs from this week's content reading, then write three of their own.

Check: Three combined sentences, each with a different joiner, in the exit ticket.

Week 2

Nominalization

Teach: Turn a verb into the noun academic texts prefer: migrate → migration, decide → decision.

Do: Hunt the content text for -tion / -ment / -ance nouns; rewrite three sentences from the "people did X" form into the "the X of…" form.

Check: Two sentences using a nominalized form correctly.

Week 3

Transitions that mean something

Teach: Replace and, also, then with however, as a result, in contrast, for example — and know which relationship each signals.

Do: Sort twelve transitions by relationship; rewrite a paragraph of the student's own writing with three precise transitions.

Check: A paragraph with three transitions, each one correct for the relationship.

Week 4

Verb forms in academic writing

Teach: Present tense for analysis, past for events, present perfect for what still matters; the -ed and -s endings that drop in speech.

Do: Edit a paragraph written in spoken register; read it aloud and hear the endings.

Check: Five verbs corrected with the reason named.

Week 5

The language of evidence

Teach: According to…, the author states that…, this shows…, which suggests… — and the difference between quoting and paraphrasing.

Do: Three evidence sentences from the content text, each with a frame, then the same three with the frame removed.

Check: One claim + one piece of evidence + one explanation, cited.

Week 6

Hedging and certainty

Teach: may, might, tends to, suggests, is likely to versus proves, always, never. Science and history both need it.

Do: Rewrite five over-certain claims; discuss what each hedge changes.

Check: Two hedged claims about this week's content.

Week 7

Cause and effect

Teach: leads to, results in, due to, consequently, because of — the structures, not just the words.

Do: Build a cause–effect chain from the content reading, then write it as a paragraph with three different structures.

Check: A three-sentence cause–effect paragraph.

Week 8

Compare and contrast

Teach: whereas, while, both… and, unlike, similarly; the two-paragraph and the point-by-point shapes.

Do: Compare two things from this week's content in a T-chart, then in a paragraph.

Check: A compare–contrast paragraph using at least four of the structures.

Week 9

The counterclaim

Teach: Some argue that…; however…; although it is true that…, …. Argument writing on the English II test lives here.

Do: Take a position from the content, write the strongest opposing view, then answer it.

Check: A claim, a counterclaim and a rebuttal in three sentences.

Week 10

Summarizing without copying

Teach: Main idea in your own words; the three-step summary; what to leave out.

Do: Summarize a content paragraph in one sentence, then a page in four.

Check: A four-sentence summary with no copied phrase over three words.

Week 11

Precision

Teach: Kill thing, stuff, a lot, good, bad, get. Replace with the content word.

Do: Students find the vague words in their own last three pieces and replace each with a precise one from the content vocabulary.

Check: A revised paragraph with zero vague words.

Week 12

Revision and the portfolio

Teach: Students compare week 1 writing with week 11, name three changes they made on purpose, and set the next goal.

Do: Portfolio conference, five minutes each; the student leads it.

Check: A one-page reflection and the next twelve-week goal.

Every week, no matter the feature

  • Grade-level text every day. No leveled substitutes for an LTEL; preview the words, read the real thing.
  • Write every day, at least a paragraph, with the feature required and named.
  • Feedback on one feature only, written in the margin as the feature's name, not a correction. The student fixes it.
  • Read it aloud. Students hear the endings and the sentence boundaries they do not see.
  • Keep the portfolio. Week 1 next to week 12 is the evidence the student, the family and the LSP review need.

For the content teacher: two moves

The clinic lives in the EL block; it only works if the content classes stop accepting the spoken register in writing. Two moves, five minutes a week to set up.

1 · Require the register, with a frame

Every written answer longer than a sentence gets a frame that forces the week's feature — the EL teacher sends it Monday. "Although ___, ___ because ___." Grade the content; mark the feature. A long-term EL who is allowed to answer in spoken English will, forever.

2 · One feature, in the margin

Pick one thing — verb endings, transitions, thing/stuff — and write only its name in the margin where it is missing. The student fixes it before the grade posts. Five seconds per paper; it is the only feedback that changes writing.

And three habits

  • Call on them. Long-term ELs have learned that silence is safe. Wait time, then the question, then wait again.
  • Grade-level text, previewed, never the easy version — they can read the easy version; it is why they are stuck.
  • Say what you see. "Your explanation of the cause was exactly right; now say it the way the textbook would." Precision is a compliment.

The student's letter (week 0)

English: You have been learning English for a long time, and you speak it well. The test says your reading and writing are the two things left. That is not a weakness — it is a map. For twelve weeks we are going to work on exactly those two things, one skill a week, and you will be able to see the change in your own writing. You are closer than anyone has told you.

Español: Llevas mucho tiempo aprendiendo inglés y lo hablas bien. El examen dice que la lectura y la escritura son las dos cosas que faltan. Eso no es una debilidad: es un mapa. Durante doce semanas vamos a trabajar exactamente en esas dos cosas, una destreza por semana, y vas a poder ver el cambio en tu propia escritura. Estás más cerca de lo que te han dicho.
