What ninety minutes can and cannot do

It cannot make a faculty bilingual, and this workshop does not pretend to. What it does is narrower and more useful: a teacher who speaks no Spanish walks out able to say a student’s name the way the student says it, greet the class, give the directions every class needs, encourage, recognize the fifteen things a student is most likely to say, and make a two-minute good-news call home from a script. Those are the moments where a Spanish-speaking student decides whether this room is for them.

It is built to be run by whoever is willing: the EL specialist, a department chair, a teacher who took Spanish in high school, or one who never did. The page plays every phrase in a Spanish voice, the timers run each round, and the facilitator notes under every table say what to do next. A Spanish-speaking colleague or student in the room is a gift, not a requirement.

Before people arrive

  • Print the packet: one facilitator script for you, one participant handout per teacher (phrase cards, pocket card, exit ticket).
  • A projector or a TV and a speaker. The page plays every phrase in a Spanish voice; test the sound before people arrive.
  • Cut the phrase cards for Round 3 (one set per table of three) — or hand each table scissors and give them the first two minutes.
  • If a Spanish-speaking colleague, paraprofessional or student is willing to sit in, invite them. They are not required; the page carries the sound.
  • Tables of three or four. Mixed departments work best — the math teacher and the English teacher hear each other's students.
  • Set a 90-minute clock where you can see it. The timers on this page run each round for you.

Block 1 · 10 minutes

Five rules that make Spanish readable

Spanish is spelled the way it sounds. Learn these five and you can read any phrase in this workshop out loud without ever having heard it.

1Five vowels, never changing

a = ah · e = eh · i = ee · o = oh · u = oo. English vowels wander; Spanish vowels do not. Say every vowel short and clean and you are already halfway there.

casa (KAH-sah) · mesa (MEH-sah) · libro (LEE-broh)

2Stress the next-to-last syllable

If a word ends in a vowel, n or s, the stress falls on the syllable before the last one. Any other ending puts the stress on the last syllable. A written accent mark wins over both.

escuela (es-KWEH-lah) · papel (pah-PEL) · página (PAH-hee-nah) · José (ho-SEH)

3h is silent, j sounds like h

hola is OH-lah. Jorge is HOR-heh. ñ is the ny in canyon (niño = NEE-nyoh). ll and y both sound like the y in yes (me llamo = meh YAH-moh).

hija (EE-hah) · Guillermo (ghee-YER-moh) · mañana (mah-NYAH-nah)

4One r taps, two rr roll

A single r is a quick tap of the tongue, like the tt in butter said fast. rr rolls. If you cannot roll it, tap it twice; everyone will understand you.

pero (PEH-roh) · perro (PEH-rroh) · Rodríguez (rroh-DREE-ghes)

5Say it with a smile

A wrong accent with a kind face lands better than silence. Students correct a brave teacher gladly; they cannot correct one who never tries.

Then say: ¿Lo dije bien? — Did I say it right? (loh DEE-heh byen)

If you are leading
  1. Open with the honest sentence: ‘Ninety minutes will not make anyone bilingual. It will give you about seventy phrases, five rules that make Spanish readable, and a call-home script. That is enough to change how a student feels in your room on Monday.’
  2. Ask who in the room already has a Spanish-speaking student. Nearly every hand goes up. That is the audience.
  3. Put the five rules on the screen one at a time. Play the example words. Everyone repeats. Spend most of the time on rule 2 — stress — because it is the one that fixes names.
  4. Set the ground rule for the room: nobody says ‘I can't.’ You say casi (almost) and try again.

Practice · Roll call, done right

Pairs. Partner A reads a name from the name card (or a real class list); Partner B says it using the stress rule. Switch after each name. Then every teacher greets three people in the room with a different greeting each time — Hola, Buenos días, Mucho gusto — and asks ¿Cómo te llamas?

If you are leading
  1. Play each phrase twice from the page, then everyone says it together. Twelve phrases, two minutes.
  2. Put the name card on the screen. Read the first four names badly on purpose — stress on the wrong syllable — then correctly. Ask the room which one they would want to hear at the door.
  3. Run the roll-call practice for five minutes. Walk the room; nobody is allowed to skip a name.
  4. Close the round: ‘Tomorrow, greet one student in Spanish at the door and say their name the way they say it. That is the whole assignment.’

Practice · Simón dice

Simon Says, in Spanish, standing up. The facilitator (or a volunteer) gives directions from the list — Simón dice: levántense… Simón dice: miren la puerta… siéntense (no Simón — anyone who sits is out). Three rounds, then a teacher who has never studied Spanish leads the last round reading from the card.

If you are leading
  1. Say every direction with the gesture you would use anyway: palm down for siéntense, hand up for levanta la mano. The gesture carries the meaning; the Spanish carries the welcome.
  2. Point out the pattern: one student ends in -a or -e, the whole class ends in -n. Escucha, escuchen. Mira, miren. They can build the plural themselves.
  3. Numbers one to thirty, counted together once, then page numbers called out and found in any book on the table.
  4. Simón dice for six minutes. Make the last leader someone who said ‘I can't do this’ at the start.

Practice · The encouragement deck

Cut the phrase cards apart. In groups of three, one teacher holds up a card in English; the other two race to say it in Spanish. Then reverse: hold up the Spanish, say the English. Keep going until every card has been said by every person.

If you are leading
  1. Explain the one sentence that matters most: Piénsalo en español. Escríbelo en inglés. It tells a Spanish-speaking student that their first language is a tool, not a problem, and that the product is still in English.
  2. Say it together five times until nobody is reading it.
  3. Run the encouragement deck for six minutes.
  4. Ask each table: which two of these will you actually say this week? Write them on the pocket card now.

Practice · Listen and answer

The facilitator plays a student line from the page (or a Spanish-speaking colleague or student says it). Teachers write what it means in English on a whiteboard or scrap paper and hold it up. Ten lines, then switch: the facilitator says the English and teachers call out the Spanish.

If you are leading
  1. This round is listening, not speaking. Teachers do not need to say these; they need to catch them.
  2. Play No entiendo first and stop. Ask: what do you do when you hear this? The answer is not ‘repeat louder.’ It is: show it, slow it, say it again with the gesture, and hand them a sentence frame.
  3. Run the listen-and-answer game for seven minutes. Keep score by table if the room wants it.
  4. Mark the two lines that are health or welfare — me duele, tengo hambre — and say who in your building those go to.

Subjects to drop into the blank

matemáticas (mah-teh-MAH-tee-kahs) · álgebra (AHL-heh-brah) · ciencias (SYEN-syahs) · biología (byoh-loh-HEE-ah) · historia (ees-TOH-ryah) · inglés (een-GLES) · educación física (eh-doo-kah-SYON FEE-see-kah) · arte (AR-teh)

Practice · Rehearse the call

Pairs, back to back so you cannot see each other, like a phone. Partner A is the teacher and reads the six-line script with a real student's name and subject. Partner B is the parent and answers only Gracias, maestra or ¿Cómo? (what?). If B says ¿Cómo?, A repeats the line slower. Switch. Time it: under two minutes each.

If you are leading
  1. Note the shift to usted: su hijo, ¿tiene?, que tenga. Parents are addressed formally. The student forms from Round 1 would sound wrong here.
  2. Subjects: matemáticas, álgebra, ciencias, biología, historia, inglés. Write yours into the blank before you rehearse.
  3. Rehearse for eight minutes. Every teacher makes the call at least twice.
  4. Say plainly: this script is for the good-news call only. Grades at risk, attendance, discipline and anything about an IEP or placement go through the district's interpreter — that is a right, not a courtesy.

The cognate shortcut

Roughly three in four academic words in English have a Spanish twin. Your Spanish speakers already own these; you just have to point.

  • problema problem
  • ejemplo example
  • importante important
  • diferente different
  • similar similar
  • experimento experiment
  • análisis analysis
  • evidencia evidence
  • resultado result
  • causa cause
  • efecto effect
  • comparar compare
  • describir describe
  • explicar explain
  • identificar identify
  • organizar organize
  • examen exam
  • proyecto project
  • laboratorio laboratory
  • texto text

Five endings that convert

-tion → -ción

education → educación · information → información

-ty → -dad

university → universidad · community → comunidad

-ly → -mente

finally → finalmente · exactly → exactamente

-ic → -ico

basic → básico · scientific → científico

-ous → -oso

famous → famoso · curious → curioso

Nine false friends

  • éxito = success (not exit)
  • librería = bookstore (not library)
  • asistir = to attend (not assist)
  • realizar = to carry out (not realize)
  • actual = current (not actual)
  • carpeta = folder (not carpet)
  • colegio = school (not college)
  • largo = long (not large)
  • embarazada = pregnant (not embarrassed)

Block 8 and after

Pocket card, exit ticket, six Tuesdays

The workshop is the easy part. The pocket card goes behind a badge; the ten-minute follow-ups keep the phrases alive for six weeks.

Closing the room
  1. Everyone fills in the pocket card: five phrases I will say this week, one student I will greet by name, one family I will call. Fold it; it fits behind a badge.
  2. Exit ticket, three lines: one phrase I can say now, one I will practice, one thing I want next. Collect them — they are the agenda for the ten-minute follow-ups.
  3. Announce the follow-up: ten minutes a week for six weeks, same room, same page. Week 1 is greet at the door. Put it on the calendar before people leave.
  4. Last word, together: Piénsalo en español. Escríbelo en inglés. Then hasta mañana.

Six ten-minute follow-ups

  1. Week 1Greet at the door

    Hola, buenos días, and every name said right.

  2. Week 2One direction a day

    Siéntense on Monday, escuchen on Tuesday, miren on Wednesday…

  3. Week 3The sentence

    Piénsalo en español. Escríbelo en inglés — on the board all week.

  4. Week 4Catch the question

    Listen for no entiendo and ¿qué página? and answer them without words first.

  5. Week 5The call

    One good-news call home, read from the script, two minutes.

  6. Week 6Ask a student to teach

    Frase de la semana: a Spanish speaker teaches the class one phrase every Monday.
