One student, two sets of rights

A dual-identified student is an English learner who also has a disability. The student is owed both language services and special education. Neither one is a substitute for the other, and neither one waits for the other.

Rule 1

English is not a disability

A student may not be found eligible for special education when the deciding factor is limited English proficiency. Second-language learning can look like a learning or speech problem. It is not one.

Rule 2

Do not wait for English

If the team suspects a disability, it evaluates. A policy such as "no referrals until two years in the program" is the thing federal reviewers have named as a violation.

Rule 3

Both services, every week

An IEP does not end EL services, and EL status does not shrink the IEP. The schedule has to show both.

The two mistakes. Schools get this wrong in both directions: referring a newcomer for special education because English is slow, and refusing to evaluate a student for years "because he's EL." This desk is built to stop both.

Language or disability?

The same classroom behavior has two possible explanations. The middle column is what it usually looks like when the cause is learning a new language. The right column is what should make the team look harder. No single row decides anything.

The strongest single question: does the difficulty show up in the home language too? A true disability does not live in only one language.

The evaluation

The evaluation has one job that is harder for an English learner: separating what the student knows from how much English the student has.

  1. Test in the language that gives the truest picture. Federal rules say assessments are given in the child's native language or other mode of communication, and in the form most likely to show what the child knows and can do, unless that is clearly not feasible.
  2. Look at both languages. For speech and language concerns, the evaluator needs to see the home language. A problem found only in English is a second-language pattern until shown otherwise.
  3. Use trained interpreters, and say so in the report. The report should state the language of each test, whether an interpreter was used, and how that limits the scores.
  4. Read standard scores with caution. Most tests were normed on English-speaking children. A low score on an English-normed test is partly an English score.
  5. Use more than tests. Observation in more than one class, work samples over time, the family interview, and progress under instruction with language supports.
  6. Rule out lack of instruction. A student who has not had appropriate instruction in reading or math, or steady schooling, has not shown a disability yet.
  7. Keep the clock. Once consent is signed, the usual evaluation timeline applies. Being an English learner does not extend it.

Five questions the EL teacher can ask the evaluator

  • In which language was each test given, and why?
  • Was an interpreter used? Trained in assessment, or a staff member who speaks the language?
  • Who was this test normed on, and what does that mean for this score?
  • What did you learn about the student's skills in the home language?
  • How does the report separate the effect of language from the suspected disability?

The meeting

For a dual-identified student, the IEP team needs someone who can speak for the language side. In most buildings that is the EL teacher.

Who has to be there

  • The parent or guardian
  • A general education teacher of the student
  • A special education teacher
  • A district representative
  • Someone who can explain the evaluation results
  • The student, when appropriate

Who makes it work for an EL

  • The EL teacher or another person with knowledge of second-language acquisition and of this student's language needs
  • An interpreter for the family, arranged by the school
  • The counselor, when schedule and credits are on the table

Two plans, one student

The student has a Language Service Plan and an IEP. Classroom teachers get both and often read neither. Fill this in once and print one page that puts the two side by side. No student name is required. Use initials if you print for a folder that travels.

Accommodations and tests

A dual-identified student can receive accommodations from both plans. They add together. The rule for every one of them is the same: it has to be written in the plan and used in class before the test window.

Exit for a dual-identified student

The exit rule is the state's rule, the same for every English learner: Level 4 or higher in each ELPT domain on the same administration (see the Exit & Monitoring Kit). An IEP team does not vote a student out of EL status, and a disability is not a reason to drop EL services while the student is still an English learner. If the disability is the reason a student cannot reach the exit score, raise it with the district EL coordinator and the District Test Coordinator. That is a state procedure question, not a building decision.

Family scripts

Short, plain lines for the first conversation, in English and Spanish. Use them with an interpreter in the room. Tap a line to hear it.

Why we want to evaluate

We see your child working hard, and we want to understand how to teach him better. We would like to do some tests, in the language he knows best.

Vemos que su hijo se esfuerza mucho, y queremos entender cómo enseñarle mejor. Nos gustaría hacerle unas evaluaciones, en el idioma que él domina mejor.

What it is not

This is not because he is learning English. Learning English is going well. We are asking about something different.

Esto no es porque está aprendiendo inglés. El inglés va bien. Estamos preguntando por algo diferente.

Your rights

Nothing happens without your written permission. You will have an interpreter at every meeting, and the papers will come in your language. It costs nothing.

No se hace nada sin su permiso por escrito. Usted tendrá un intérprete en cada reunión, y los documentos llegarán en su idioma. No cuesta nada.

What we need from you

You know your child best. Please tell us what you see at home, in your language: how he talks, how he remembers, how he learned as a small child.

Usted conoce a su hijo mejor que nadie. Por favor, cuéntenos lo que ve en casa, en su idioma: cómo habla, cómo recuerda las cosas, cómo aprendió de pequeño.

Both services

If he qualifies, he keeps his English class and also gets the new help. He does not lose one to get the other.

Si califica, sigue con su clase de inglés y además recibe la nueva ayuda. No pierde una para recibir la otra.
