Exit is a date. Monitoring is four years.

Leaving EL status is the goal of the program, and the day it happens the school's job changes, it does not end. This kit covers the three parts: getting the exit right, watching the student for four years, and acting when a monitored student starts to slip.

The exit rule

Level 4 in every domain

In Mississippi a student exits with Level 4 or higher in each ELPT domain, on the same administration. Grades, teacher opinion and years in the program do not exit a student, and they do not hold one back.

The watch

Four years, every nine weeks

After exit the school monitors academic progress for four years. Laurel's plan reviews grades and assessments every nine weeks. Sixteen checks, on one form.

The response

Ask one question first

When a monitored student struggles, the first question is whether language is part of the cause. If it is, the school owes language support again, and can rescreen.

The response ladder →

The year, in order

  1. Spring. The student takes the ELPT in the state window.
  2. Scores arrive. Check all four domain levels against the exit rule. Print the report.
  3. Decision and date. The team records the exit and dates it.
  4. Notice. The family is told in their language. The student's status is changed in the data systems.
  5. Hand-off. Next year's teachers and the counselor are told who is in monitoring.
  6. Years 1 to 4. A check every nine weeks. A concern starts the response ladder the same week.
  7. End of Year 4. Monitoring closes. The file is kept with the cumulative record.
Where this comes from. ESSA §3113(b)(2) (standardized statewide entrance and exit procedures) and §3121(a)(5) (reporting on former English learners for each of the four years after exit); the January 2015 Dear Colleague Letter from the U.S. Departments of Education and Justice, section on exiting and monitoring (retest and re-offer services when a former English learner struggles because of language); the Mississippi State Board of Education exit standard adopted June 19, 2025; the Laurel School District English Learner Plan, SY25–26 (four years, nine-week review). The exit checklist

Ten steps between the score report and a clean exit. Most audit findings on exit are not wrong decisions. They are a missing letter, a missing date, or a status that was never changed.

Three things that are not exit. A parent waiving services (the student is still an English learner and still takes the ELPT). A student with strong grades and a Level 3 in Writing (still an English learner). A senior graduating (leaves as an English learner, not as exited).
The near miss. A student with three 4s and one 3 has a one-domain job for next year. Put the domain in the LSP goal, and see the Long-Term EL Clinic if the same domain has held the student for years.

The monitoring form

Enter the school year the student exited. The form lays out all four years, a row for every nine weeks, ready to print and keep in the folder. Use initials if the page will travel.

What to look at, each nine weeks

Five minutes per student

  • Grades in the four core classes
  • Attendance and discipline entries
  • Any benchmark or state-test score since the last check
  • One line from a core teacher: "holding" or "concern"

A concern is any one of these

  • A core grade below C, or a drop of a full letter
  • A failed benchmark in a tested subject
  • Attendance sliding
  • A teacher saying the reading or writing load is the problem

When a monitored student slips

A drop in grades after exit is common, and most of the time it is ordinary: a harder course, a new school, a hard year. The school's duty is to find out whether language is part of it, quickly, and to write down what it did.

  1. Same week: talk to the student and the teacher. Which class, which tasks? "I understand the lesson but I can't write the essay" is a language answer. "I'm not turning work in" is usually not.
  2. Look at the work. One long reading and one piece of writing from the class in question. Is the gap in the content or in the academic English it is delivered in?
  3. Classroom supports first. Give the teacher two or three specific moves: a model answer, sentence frames for the writing task, vocabulary taught before the reading. Check again in three weeks.
  4. Then the school's intervention system. If the concern continues, the student enters the same tiered support any student would get, with the EL teacher at the table.
  5. If language is the cause, re-offer language support. Federal guidance is direct on this point: when a former English learner is struggling because of a remaining language need, the school retests and offers services again. Start with the district EL coordinator.
  6. Tell the family. In their language, what you saw and what the school is doing.
  7. Write it on the form. Date, concern, action, result. A monitoring file with no entries looks the same as no monitoring.

Rescreening and re-entry. How a former English learner is retested and returned to EL status is a state and district procedure. Confirm the current Mississippi steps with your district EL coordinator before you promise a family anything. Do not wait on that answer to give classroom support.

Two short documents. Fill in the blanks and print. The family letter is in English and Spanish; for other languages, see the hub's parent letters.

To the family · English

Dear family of __________,

We have good news. On this year's English Language Proficiency Test, your child scored Level 4 or higher in listening, speaking, reading and writing. That meets Mississippi's standard for English proficiency. Your child is no longer classified as an English learner and will not take the test again.

For the next four years, the school will check your child's grades and progress every nine weeks. If your child needs help, we will contact you and provide support. You may call us at any time with questions.

Congratulations to your child, and to you.

For classroom teachers

Your roster says "monitored" or "former EL" next to a name. Here is what that means in your room.

What it means

  • The student met the state's English standard in all four domains.
  • There is no LSP and there are no EL test accommodations.
  • The school is checking grades every nine weeks for four years.
  • You are the early warning. The EL teacher sees a grade weeks after you see the work.

What it does not mean

  • It does not mean academic English is finished. The language of a tenth-grade textbook keeps growing for years.
  • It does not mean supports are off limits. A model answer and a word bank are good teaching for anyone.
  • It does not mean a struggle is "not a language thing." Ask.
Three moves that cost nothing. Show a finished example before a long writing task. Teach five words before the hardest reading of the week. Let the student talk the answer through with a partner before writing it.
When to send a note: a full letter-grade drop, two missing major assignments, or your own sense that the reading or writing load is the wall. One line to the EL teacher is enough.
