Caffeine and the Teen Brain

That energy drink is not giving you energy. It is hiding your tiredness, and the bill comes due at night.

1The 3:00 p.m. wall

Dani hits a wall every day at 3:00 p.m. Her eyes feel heavy in seventh period. Her head drops. So on the way to practice she buys a 16-ounce energy drink. Ten minutes later she feels great. By 9:00 p.m. she feels tired again, but when she lies down at 11:00, she cannot fall asleep. The next day she is even more tired. So she buys another one. Dani is in a , and she is not alone. 2What is caffeine, and where is it hiding?

Caffeine is a natural chemical found in coffee beans, tea leaves, and cacao. It is also added to many drinks. A can of soda has about 35 milligrams (mg). A cup of coffee has about 95 mg. A typical 16-ounce energy drink has about 160 mg, and some have more than 300 mg. The American Academy of Pediatrics that teenagers consume no more than 100 mg of caffeine a day. That is less than one energy drink. Many students drink two.

3How caffeine tricks your brain

Here is a surprise: caffeine does not give you energy. Food gives you energy. Caffeine only hides your tiredness. All day, your brain makes a chemical called adenosine. The more adenosine builds up, the sleepier you feel. Caffeine has almost the same shape as adenosine, so it the spots where adenosine is supposed to fit. Your brain cannot feel the tiredness, so you feel awake. But the adenosine is still there, waiting. When the caffeine , all of that tiredness hits at once. People call this a .

4The 4:00 p.m. problem

Caffeine stays in your body for a long time. Its half-life, the time it takes for half of it to leave, is about five to six hours in a teenager. That means if Dani drinks 160 mg of caffeine at 4:00 p.m., about 80 mg is still in her body at 10:00 p.m., and 40 mg is still there at 4:00 a.m. Her brain is trying to sleep with the equivalent of a can of soda still running through it. This is why sleep experts say caffeine should be after lunch. The teen brain already wants to stay up late. Caffeine pushes it even later.

5What too much caffeine does

Small amounts of caffeine are safe for most adults. For a growing teen, large amounts cause problems. First, it affects the heart. Too much caffeine causes a fast heartbeat and, in rare cases, dangerous heart rhythms. Second, it affects mood. Caffeine can make a person feel , nervous, or anxious. Third, it causes headaches and stomachaches, especially when a person tries to stop. Finally, it affects sleep, and poor sleep hurts memory, attention, and grades. Energy drinks are a special concern because they are often consumed quickly, in large amounts, and mixed with sugar.

6Breaking the cycle

Dani does not need to quit caffeine forever. She needs a better plan. Sleep experts suggest three steps. One: move caffeine to the morning only, and keep it under 100 mg. Two: when the 3:00 p.m. wall hits, try the that actually work: a glass of water, a short walk, a snack with protein, or ten minutes of sunlight. Three: fix the real problem. The reason Dani is tired at 3:00 is that she went to sleep at midnight. An earlier bedtime does more than any drink can. Caffeine treats the . Sleep treats the cause.
